## Profit, Cash and Credit
*Profit versus cash in hand, budgeting, saving, and the true cost of borrowing*

**Profit is not cash in hand.** Profit is what is left after your costs, worked out on paper. Cash in hand is the credits you can spend today. A trader can show a good profit and still be short of cash, because ore sitting in the cargo hold is not credits until it is sold. Your receipts log in the filing cabinet shows the money in and the money out, so check it before you count your profit.

**Budgeting: income minus costs.** A budget is simple: your income minus your costs. Income comes from your sales, your auctions and the Bank's desks. Costs include the lots you buy, any fees such as the 2.5% auction fee or the printing fees, loan payments, and the food and supplies you need. Plan the week before you place your orders, so you know what you can afford.

**Needs first, then wants.** A need is something your work depends on, such as the supplies that keep you working. A want is nice to have, such as a new uniform from the closet. Pay for your needs first, and only spend on wants from what is left. When money is tight, a want is the first thing to wait for.

**A cash reserve for hard weeks.** A cash reserve is money set aside for things that go wrong: a bad week of prices, a repair, or a fee you did not expect. A common goal is enough to cover a few weeks of your costs. The savings account is a good place for it, because you can take money out at any time, and the Bank's savings goal for an emergency fund keeps you on track. Real households and businesses do the same thing.

**The cost of borrowing.** Interest is the price of money. Savings and term deposits earn it for you, at the Bank's rates. A loan charges it to you, at 6 to 12 percent a week, worked out on what you still owe. Before you sign, the Bank shows the total cost of borrowing and what the purchase is expected to earn. A loan of 1,000 credits at 6 percent a week over 4 weeks costs about 289 credits a week, or 1,156 in all, so borrowing costs 156 credits.

**Paying early, and why interest grows.** Interest added to your balance then earns interest too, which is compound growth. Saving and reinvesting your earnings lets it grow faster over time. Paying a loan off early saves interest, because the Bank only charges interest on what is still owed up to the day you pay. Missed payments bring a late fee and lower your credit standing, so pay on time.

## Money by the Numbers
*Budgets, saving, loans, scams and records, one step at a time*

**A budget is a simple program.** A budget works like a simple program. The input is the credits that come in, from your work, trades and sales, and the costs are what goes out. The result is what is left over; if it is below zero, cut a cost or find more income before your next payment is due. Keep a record of every credit in and out, like a program's log, and check it against your receipts.

**Needs, wants and savings.** Pay for your needs first: food and drink, a bed to rest in, and the repairs your craft needs to fly safely. Next, move a set amount into savings before you spend on anything else, and keep an emergency fund for surprises such as a sudden repair. Wants, like a new colour for your uniform, come last and only from what is left. A savings goal names what you are saving for, such as a new ship, and shows how far you have got.

**Compound interest, one step at a time.** Compound interest means the interest you earn is added to your balance, so the next interest payment is worked out on a bigger amount. Take 10,000 credits at the Bank's savings rate of 0.5% a week: after one week it is 10,050, after two weeks 10,100.25, and after four weeks 10,201.51. Simple interest, worked out only on the first 10,000, would give 10,200 after four weeks, so the gap is small at first but grows to about 12,960 against 12,600 after a year. The Bank's savings account adds your earned interest to the balance each time you deposit or take money out, so these figures show how compounding works rather than the exact figures your account shows.

**When a loan makes sense.** A loan is money you borrow and pay back with interest, which is the fee for using it. The Bank's loans cost 6 to 12 percent a week, depending on your repayment record, far more than a savings account earns. A loan makes sense only when what it helps you earn is more than what it costs, such as a machine that brings in more each week than its weekly payment. Paying early saves interest, because the early-payoff amount is what you owe plus interest only up to that day; missed payments bring a late fee and lower your credit standing.

**Too good to be true.** An offer that promises a big, safe gain with no risk, and a deadline to act now, is the classic sign of a scam. Honest savings grow slowly and steadily, and a real bank states its rates and its risks plainly. In the game, players never message each other, so any message that claims to come from another player is not real. Walk away from anyone who asks for an up-front fee to release money, however friendly they sound.

**Protect your account and keep receipts.** Your sign-in code is a six-digit number sent to your phone or email each time you sign in. Never share it, and type it only into the sign-in screen, even if someone says they work for the game or the Bank. Every transfer of goods gives you a receipt with a code that anyone can check, so keep your receipts and save copies of the important ones to your own device.

## Costing a Build
*What a build really costs, and how to pay for it*

**What a build costs.** Every build has three kinds of cost. Materials are the raw materials you take from the cargo hold, at what they cost you on the market. Labour is the time your crew spends on the job, and it counts even when no credits change hands. Printing fees are what the Starship Bank charges to print copies of a design: 100 credits once, with a design's first run, and then 10 credits for each copy.

**Cost per copy.** Add up everything a run costs, then divide by the number of copies. Take a first run of 25 copies. The setup fee is 100, and the copies come to 25 times 10, so the printing alone is 350 credits. That is 14 a copy. Materials and labour for each copy come on top. The setup fee is paid only once, so the bigger the run, the less of it each copy carries.

**Saving or borrowing.** A production plant costs a lot before it earns anything. You can save up for it, or borrow from the Bank and build now. Saving costs you time, but no interest. Your savings grow at the Bank's savings rate, and a term deposit pays more the longer you lock it up. Take a term deposit out early and you give up its interest, so choose the term before you start.

**What borrowing really costs.** Take a loan of 1,000 credits at 8% a week, paid back over 8 weeks, and each weekly payment is 175 credits. That comes to 1,400 in all, so the loan costs 400 in interest. Interest is charged only on what you still owe, so every payment clears some interest and the rest cuts the debt. Before you sign, the Bank shows you the total cost and what the purchase should earn. Ask one question: will it pay for itself?

**Payback time.** Payback time is the cost divided by what the build earns each week after its running costs. A plant that costs 1,000 and earns 250 a week pays back in 4 weeks. With a loan, the weekly payments come out of that same money, so the loan is paid first. Paying a loan off early saves interest: the payoff is what you still owe, plus interest only up to that day.

**Upkeep and records.** Set money aside each week for upkeep, before anything breaks. A machine that stops costs you output, and a repair you must borrow for at 6 to 12% a week costs far more than the same repair paid from savings. Pay every loan payment on its date: a late payment adds a fee and lowers your credit standing. Keep every receipt in the receipts log in your Private Quarters, and check your numbers against them.

## A Pilot's Money
*The cost of flying, saving for a ship, borrowing wisely and a repair fund*

**What flying really costs.** Every flight costs more than fuel. Your time is the first cost: a long run keeps you from other work. Your body pays too, because each of your three bars drains about 2.2 points an hour while you are awake, and your bed is the fastest way to win them back. Your craft wears out like any machine, so it needs upkeep, which is called maintenance: checking and servicing it before it breaks.

**Income minus costs.** Budgeting comes down to one simple sum: what comes in, minus what goes out. Income is what you earn from trades, auctions and the Bank's desks. Costs include fuel, food, maintenance and the weekly payments on any loan. Every transfer gives you a receipt in your filing cabinet, so you can check where the money really went.

**Needs and wants.** A need keeps you flying and safe: fuel, food and the repairs that keep your craft fit to fly. A want is nice to have. Pay for your needs first, and decide on wants with what is left. Some foods give a quick burst and then a crash, and a crash can cost you more rest later, so a treat is a want, not a need.

**Saving, and the interest it earns.** Saving means setting money aside for a goal, such as a bigger hold or a new ship, before you spend it. Interest is money the Bank pays you for keeping your credits with it. A savings account pays a small rate and lets you take money out at any time. A term deposit locks your money for 2, 4 or 8 weeks for a higher rate, but if you close it early you lose all the interest it would have earned.

**Borrowing: when a loan pays for itself.** A loan lets you have something now and pay for it later, with interest on what you still owe. Before you sign, the Bank shows you the total you will pay back and the cost of borrowing. Ask one question: will this earn back more than it costs? A bigger hold that brings back more ore on each trip can pay for itself. A loan for treats earns nothing back, so every payment comes out of your income.

**Paying off early, and an emergency fund.** Paying a loan off early saves interest, because the early payoff is only what you still owe plus the interest up to that day. A missed payment brings a late fee, and your credit standing with the Bank falls, which makes the next loan harder to get. An emergency fund is money kept aside for surprises, such as a broken part, so a repair does not force you to borrow at a high rate.

## Health, Wealth and Choices
*Budgets, savings and loans, and how money choices touch your health*

**Income minus costs.** A budget is simple arithmetic: what comes in, minus what goes out. Credits come in from trades, auctions and the Bank's market desks. Costs go out for food, supplies and the gear your work needs, so check the sum before you spend, and write it down once a week.

**Needs and wants.** A need is something your work and your health depend on, such as a wholesome meal, enough sleep and gear that keeps you safe. A want is nice to have, so pay for the needs first. A cheap-looking choice can cost you later: an energy drink lifts you fast, but both energy and focus then drain faster for hours, and your next sleep refills you more slowly. Food never replaces sleep.

**Saving and the emergency fund.** Saving means setting aside part of what you earn now, so you can use it later. The Bank's savings account pays a little interest and lets you take your credits out at any time. An emergency fund is money kept for surprises, such as an injury, an illness or broken gear, when you cannot work for a while. The Bank's savings goals include one called An emergency fund, so you can watch it grow.

**Interest earned and interest paid.** Interest is the price of money over time. When you deposit credits, the Bank pays you interest; when you borrow, you pay it. A term deposit pays more than a savings account, and the longer you lock it away, the higher its rate, but the interest is paid only at the end of the term. The Bank's screen shows the current rates before you choose.

**Borrowing well, and paying early.** A loan makes sense when what it buys earns more than it costs. The Bank's quote shows the weekly payment, the total you will pay back and the expected return, so ask "will it pay for itself?" before you sign. Loan rates run from 6% to 12% a week, and a weaker repayment record means a higher rate. Paying off early saves the interest that has not been charged yet, so use spare credits to clear the loan first.

**Receipts and steady money.** Every transfer of credits gives you a receipt with a code you can check, so keep them. They show where your money went and help you check your budget. A late payment adds a fee to what you owe and lowers your credit standing, which makes later borrowing harder. Money worry is a health cost too: stress can keep you awake, and poor sleep makes stamina and focus fall, so a simple, calm money plan protects your body as well as your credits.

## A Miner's Income
*What a trip earns against what it costs, and how to make the money last*

**Income minus costs.** A miner's profit is the income from a trip minus the costs of making it. Costs are everything that goes out: loan payments, food and drink, and the upkeep of your kit, as in real life. Write both down each week, and keep your receipts, because every transfer of goods gives you one and they are kept in your filing cabinet. Credits in the game are for play only and never turn into real money.

**Prices rise and fall.** Ore is sold on a market where buyers and sellers set the price, and the Starship bank trades there too. Prices move with what is waiting to be bought and sold, and they lean back toward a reference price over time. Every item has a floor, and nothing trades below it: copper ore is listed at 600 credits a 100 kg lot, with a floor of 300. A high price this week does not promise one next week.

**Saving through the lean weeks.** A miner's income comes in lumps, with a big haul one week and a thin one the next. A savings account smooths this out: it earns 0.5% of its value each week, and you can take money out at any time. Keep an emergency fund there, enough to cover your costs for several weeks, so a thin week never forces you to sell in a hurry. Savings goals, such as a new ship, tell you what you are saving toward.

**Interest earned and interest paid.** Interest works both ways. The Bank pays you on deposits: 0.5% a week in a savings account, and 1% to 1.5% a week in a term deposit locked for 2, 4 or 8 weeks. A term deposit pays its interest only when the term ends in full, so closing it early returns only what you put in. A loan costs 6% to 12% a week on what you still owe, depending on your record with the Bank.

**When a loan pays for itself.** Borrowing for a bigger hold makes sense only when the extra income it brings, after the weekly payments, is more than the loan costs. Before you sign, the Bank shows you the total cost of the loan, and you should ask what the purchase will really earn. Borrowing for something that earns nothing is a cost, not an investment. A missed payment adds a late fee to what you owe and lowers your credit standing, which makes your next loan dearer. Paying a loan off early saves the interest you would have paid later.

**Do not sell at the bottom.** When prices are low and you need cash, it is tempting to sell everything at once. That locks in the lowest price on the day you were most pressed. A limit order names the lowest price you will accept, and selling in lots spreads your sales over time. An emergency fund is what lets you wait for a fair price.

## Charting a Course to Your Goals
*Budgets, savings goals, interest and loans, planned like a voyage*

**Income minus costs.** Every voyage starts with two numbers: what comes in and what goes out. Your income is the credits you earn, from sales on the market, from auctions and from the Bank's desks. Your costs are everything you pay out, such as supplies, repairs and loan payments. Your budget is income minus costs, and it tells you whether you are moving forward or drifting back.

**Needs and wants.** A need is something you cannot do without, such as the food and supplies that keep your crew working. A want is something nice that you could live without, such as a new coat for your closet. Pay for your needs first, every time. Then see what is left, and decide which wants fit inside it.

**A goal is a destination.** A goal turns saving into a trip with an end. Name what you are saving for, set a target, and note the date you hope to arrive. The Starship Bank lets you set savings goals for a new ship, a production plant, printing your blueprints, an emergency fund or retirement on the New Earth. Everything you hold at the Bank counts toward the goal.

**Plotting the route.** To plan the trip, take the target and subtract what you already have. Divide what is left by how much you can put aside each week, and you have the number of weeks to go. For example, a target of 3,000 credits with 600 saved, and 200 put aside each week, is 2,400 divided by 200: twelve weeks. Check your position every week, and write down what you saved, so you can see whether the route still holds.

**Interest: money that works both ways.** Interest is the price of money. When you save with the Bank, your savings earn interest, at half a percent a week in a savings account. A term deposit earns more, but the money is locked for 2, 4 or 8 weeks, and taking it out early gives up the interest. When you borrow, the interest is what you pay, so the longer money is owed, the more it costs.

**Loans, early payoff and course corrections.** A loan makes sense when what it lets you do earns more than it costs. Before you sign, the Bank shows you the total cost and the weekly payments. Paying off early saves the interest that has not yet been charged, and a missed payment brings a late fee and lowers your standing. Keep a small emergency fund so one bad week does not knock you off course, and if you fall behind, spend less on wants, earn more, or move the date of the goal.

## Budgeting a Research Project
*Planning a budget, tracking it, and thinking about money the way you think about an experiment*

**Plan the budget first.** A budget starts with two lists: what comes in and what goes out. Write it down before you spend a single credit, just as you write down a guess before you test it. Put the needs first, such as the ore lots and parts your experiment cannot run without, and let wants like a new uniform from the closet wait. Credits here are for the game only and never turn into real money.

**Measure, compare, adjust.** Track what you actually spend, and check it against the plan, the way you measure an experiment. Keep a record of every purchase: the receipts log in your filing cabinet holds a copy of each transfer. Compare the total you spent with the plan for the same week. If you are over, adjust the next week's plan instead of hoping it will fix itself.

**Saving and long-term goals.** Money you do not need yet can grow in savings. The Starship Bank's savings account pays 0.5% interest a week, and you may take money out at any time. Savings goals name what you are saving for, such as a new ship, a production plant or an emergency fund, and show how close you are. Some big goals take years of steady saving, so small amounts put aside each week matter more than one lucky trade.

**Interest earned and interest paid.** Interest is the price of money. When you put credits in the Bank, the Bank pays you interest, and when you borrow, you pay it. A term deposit locks your credits for 2, 4 or 8 weeks and pays more, at 1, 1.25 or 1.5% a week, paid when the term ends. Close it early and you get back only what you put in, so the interest is lost.

**When a loan makes sense.** A loan is worth taking when what you buy earns back more than the loan costs. At 6% a week, a 1,000-credit loan over 4 weeks costs about 156 credits in interest, so the thing you buy must bring back more than that. Loans to a borrower with a weak record cost up to 12% a week, and supplies that are used up and bring nothing back are the hardest to justify. If you cannot see how the money pays itself back, do not borrow it.

**Paying off early and the safety fund.** Paying a loan off early saves the interest for the weeks that would still have been charged, and the Bank works out the payoff from today. A missed payment brings a late fee of 5% of the payment, added to what you owe, and it lowers your standing. Keep an emergency fund in savings, so an unexpected cost does not force a costly loan. In real life, many people aim for several months of basic costs.
