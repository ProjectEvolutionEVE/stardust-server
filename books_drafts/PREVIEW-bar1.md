# Flight · Pilot

## Flight Controls and Handling
*Throttle, stick and thrusters, and how a craft really moves in space*

**Three controls.** Your craft has three main controls. The throttle, the arrows in the bottom right corner under WARP, sets how fast you may go. The stick works anywhere on the screen: press and hold to steer. The side thrusters at the bottom left turn the craft where it sits.

**The throttle.** Each tap of the up arrow adds 10%, and each tap of the down arrow takes 10% away, from 0% to 100%. Under power, the craft speeds up toward the speed the throttle sets. Turn the throttle down and it coasts down to the new speed. At 0% it stops pushing and drifts to a stop.

**Steering with the stick.** Press and hold anywhere away from the buttons, and the stick appears under your finger. Pull back to raise the nose, push to dip it, and move left or right to bank. Under power, the craft flies where it points.

**Turning in place.** The side thrusters turn the craft without moving it forward. Use them to line up on a target before you take aim with the stick. They are for turning, not for going anywhere.

**Newton's laws in space.** Newton's first law says a moving thing keeps moving at the same speed and in the same direction unless a force acts on it. Space has almost no air, so nothing slows a craft down: once you stop pushing, you keep going. Newton's second law says the change in speed depends on the push divided by the mass, so a heavy craft speeds up more slowly than a light one with the same engine.

**Why the game keeps your speed.** The game is not an exact copy of empty space. Left alone, a craft would drift forever, so the game gently slows it when you are not pushing, and the throttle gives you a speed you can count on. Think of the throttle as a speed setting, not a rocket that never stops.

## Instruments and the HUD
*The scanner, the gold rim, the hold bar and the radar*

**Reading the screen.** A status line at the bottom names what the scanner is doing. A thin bar under it fills as the scanner picks up a return. A large, faint number shows how far away whatever the scanner is tracking. A thin red bar under the HOLD button fills as your hold fills.

**The scanner's hiss and tone.** Listen first. A soft hiss means nothing is in the sweep. When you point at a debris field, a calm tone rises out of the hiss. It gets higher and clearer the closer you aim and the nearer you get, and dead centre the static falls away.

**Bearings and locks.** Point at a return to take a bearing, and the game says BEARING TAKEN with a short ping. Move off to one side and sweep the same return again. Where the two bearings cross, the field is fixed, the scanner locks it and a two-note chime sounds. A close, strong field can be fixed from a single bearing.

**The gold rim cue.** While you hold the stick, a faint gold glow appears on its rim. It points toward the nearest debris field you have not yet cleared, even when you cannot see or hear it. When the whole rim glows, the field is dead ahead.

**The radar.** Pull down the RADAR tab at the top of the screen to drop the red scope. A red sweep turns once every 12 seconds, with a glow trailing about one second behind it. Straight up on the scope is the way your nose points. Tap the scope to change its range: 5 km for nearby fields, 75 km for a ring's band, 2 AU for the inner system and 11 AU for the whole system out to Saturn.

**Labels and distances.** Landmarks carry labels with their names and distances, so you can match what you see with the map. Use the distance to plan a warp or a flight. Pieces that are too big for the hold are not taken aboard, so check the hold bar before you collect.

## Navigating the Solar System
*Distances, the four rings, the gas giants and the wormhole*

**Measuring space.** Distances between planets are too large for kilometres to be handy. An astronomical unit (AU) is the average distance from the Earth to the Sun: 149,597,870.7 km. Sunlight takes about eight minutes to cover one AU, so the whole solar system is measured in AU to keep the numbers easy to compare.

**The four rings.** Four bands of debris lie where the inner planets once orbited: Mercury at 0.387 AU, Venus at 0.723 AU, Earth at 1.0 AU and Mars at 1.524 AU. Each band is about 50 km wide across its line and about 6 km thick above and below. Those four planets were destroyed, and what is left of them is what you collect.

**Jupiter and Saturn.** Jupiter lies 5.204 AU from the Sun, and Saturn 9.583 AU, far beyond the rings. Both are gas giants, much larger than the Earth, and Saturn has a bright ring of its own. Your starter craft can warp to either one.

**The wormhole and the Starship.** About 3 million km beyond Jupiter, away from the Sun, a wormhole glows with a ring of light. The Starship, the WSC Alexander von Humboldt, is parked 30 km off it with its bow pointing toward the Sun. To come home, warp to the Starship, then double-tap it and the autopilot brings you in to dock.

**Why WARP is needed.** At true scale, the distances are enormous. Flying beyond about 200 km would take far too long, so WARP crosses them in seconds. Even light takes about 43 minutes to reach Jupiter, so the scale is real and not something to fly across by hand.

**Reading your position.** Labels name the landmarks and show how far away they are. The info line shows your speed and your distance from the Sun in AU, and it tells you which ring you are in. A distance of 1.000 AU means you are at the Earth's average distance from the Sun.

## Orbits in Plain Words
*Why things keep going round the Sun, and how long it takes*

**Falling round the Sun.** An orbit is a fall that keeps missing. The Sun's gravity pulls on everything in the solar system, but each object moves sideways fast enough that it keeps falling round the Sun instead of into it. Gravity bends the path, and speed keeps it going.

**Kepler's first law: an oval.** In the early 1600s, Johannes Kepler worked out that planets move in ovals called ellipses. The Sun sits near one end of the oval rather than at its centre. Most planets are close to circles, but the shape is still an ellipse.

**Kepler's second law: faster up close.** A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. So a planet moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun and slowest when it is farthest away. Speed rises and falls all the way round the orbit.

**Kepler's third law: distance sets the year.** The square of an orbit's time is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun. Double the distance and the year becomes about 2.8 times longer. That is why the inner planets race round and the outer giants crawl.

**Real years around the Sun.** Mercury takes about 88 days to orbit the Sun, Venus about 225 days, Earth 365 days and Mars about 687 days. Jupiter takes about 11.9 years and Saturn about 29.5 years. These times closely match the distances, as the third law says.

**What it means for flying.** Real craft cannot simply speed up to reach a place sooner. A change in speed bends the path, so mission planners work out every burn ahead of time using these rules. That is why a pilot plans the path as well as the speed.

## Pilot Safety and Your Body
*Your three bars, rest in bed or in the cockpit, and staying safe in space*

**Your three bars.** Your character has three bars, each from 0 to 100: stamina, energy and mental focus. Each one drains while you are awake, by about 2.2 points an hour. A full 16-hour day takes a bar from 100% down to about 65%.

**What the bars mean.** At 65% you are at the lowest recommended level. Below that, your abilities begin to decline, and below 50% you can no longer carry out your duties properly. Studying and flying get harder as the bars fall, and the game warns you when you are pushing past healthy limits.

**Rest in your bed.** Your bed in the Private Quarters gives the best rest. Each bar refills 35 points every 6 hours, so 6 hours takes you from 65% back to 100%. Starting from 50%, it takes about 8 and a half hours to be fully rested.

**Rest in the cockpit.** You can also sleep in your craft's cockpit, out in space. It refills at half the rate of your bed, so 35 points takes about 12 hours. Use it when you must, but your bed is always the better rest.

**Why tired pilots fly worse.** Tiredness slows reactions and dulls attention, for real pilots and for real astronauts alike. A tired pilot misreads a reading or takes a bearing badly. Rest is part of flying, not a break from it.

**Staying safe in space.** Sunlight gets much stronger as you get closer to the Sun. The inverse-square rule says that at Mercury's distance it is about 6.7 times stronger than at Earth's. Space also carries radiation from the Sun and from deep space, which is why real spacecraft are shielded. Astronauts follow strict sleep schedules too, because tired crews make mistakes.

# Business · Trader

## Lots, Floors and Fees
*The smallest orders, the lowest prices, and the fees you will pay*

**What a lot is.** A lot is the smallest order you can place. Prices are whole credits per lot. Iron ore and copper ore trade in 100 kg lots, silver ore in 10 kg lots, and native gold and industrial diamond in 1 kg lots. Your order always counts in whole lots.

**Why lots are sized this way.** Goods with wide price swings get bigger minimum orders, so there are no really small orders. Ore that is less than one lot waits in your space in the cargo hold until you have a full lot to sell.

**Floor prices.** Each item has a floor price, and nothing trades below it. In the current price list, the floor is half of the starting price. For example, iron ore starts at 200 credits per 100 kg lot, and its floor is 100 credits.

**The auction fee.** When a blueprint copy sells at auction, the winner pays the winning bid plus a 2.5% Starship fee on top. The fee is rounded up to whole credits and goes to the WSC, the governing body. The designer receives the full winning bid.

**Printing fees.** A blueprint is printed in runs. The first run of a design costs a one-time setup fee of 100 credits, plus 10 credits for each copy. Later runs cost 10 credits for each copy. Printing credits go to the Starship Bank. So a first run of 25 copies costs 350 credits.

**Pricing your copies.** A designer sets a floor for the copies they auction. Setting it above what each copy cost is how a designer makes a profit. A floor below cost is allowed only after you tick a warning box that confirms you know you are selling below your costs.

## Reading the Order Book
*Bids, asks, limit orders and the instant buy and sell*

**Two lists.** The order book has two lists. Bids are offers to buy, and asks are offers to sell. The best bid is the highest price a buyer will pay. The best ask is the lowest price a seller will take. The best price always comes first.

**Limit orders.** A limit order names your price. A limit bid buys at that price or lower, and it waits if no one is selling there yet. A limit ask sells at that price or higher. Your credits or your goods are set aside as soon as you place the order, and cancelling gives them back.

**Buy now and Sell now.** Buy now and Sell now take the best orders already waiting, one price after another. They never wait. A trade fills at the price of the order that was already waiting, so you know what you will pay before you press the button.

**Nobody is named.** Traders are never named. The book shows prices and amounts, not who is on the other side. Players never talk to each other, so you learn only from the prices and the trades you can see.

**The Starship bank on the book.** The Starship bank trades on this market too. Its buy desk and sell desk keep a standing bid 10% under and a standing ask 10% over the last price, so you can always sell and always buy. The two desks trade with each other and with players.

**Reading the trades.** Each trade shows its price, its quantity and the time it happened. When the price rises across several trades, the market is climbing. Traders call this running list of trades the tape, and it tells you more than one trade alone.

## Keeping Good Records
*Receipts, profit and a simple trade log*

**Why records matter.** A trader who keeps good records knows what they paid, what they sold for, and what is still on the table. Without records, a lucky trade and a good trade look the same. Merchants have kept records for thousands of years for exactly this reason.

**Receipts.** Every transfer of goods gives you a receipt with a code that anyone can check. Your receipts log lives in the filing cabinet in your Private Quarters. Keep it. It is the proof of what happened, and it is the first thing to check when a number looks wrong.

**Credits exist only in the game.** Credits come from play, and they exist only inside the game. They never come from real money and never turn into real money. So your trade log records game credits only, and it is a record of the game, not of your savings.

**Cost and profit.** Write down each buy: the item, the number of lots, the price and the date. When you sell, your profit is what you received minus what you paid, less any fees. For example, if you buy one silver ore lot for 300 credits and sell it for 340, you made 40 credits before fees.

**A simple trade log.** Keep a log with one line for each trade: the date, the item, the lots, the price, the total and any fee. Check the log against your receipts from time to time. If the two do not match, find out why before you trust either one.

**Good records, good decisions.** When you know your numbers, you can tell whether an item is worth holding or selling, and you can plan your next order. Good records let you tell a good trade from a lucky one, and that is how a trader gets better over time.

## Risk and Patience
*Spreading your credits, keeping calm, and waiting for your price*

**What risk means.** Risk is the chance that a trade goes against you. Prices can fall after you buy and rise after you sell. Every market has risk. A good trader plans for it instead of hoping it will not happen.

**Do not put everything in one item.** The market lists more than one item, so you can spread your credits across them. If one price goes the wrong way, the others may not, and one bad trade will not sink your whole account.

**Do not panic-sell.** When a price drops fast, it is tempting to sell at once just to stop the feeling. Selling in a panic often means selling at the lowest point. The market leans prices back toward a reference price, so a sudden drop is not always the end.

**Buy low and sell high is harder than it sounds.** Everyone wants to buy low and sell high. But no one rings a bell at the bottom. You can usually tell a price was low or high only when you look back. Decide your price before you place the order, and stick to it.

**Patience with limit orders.** A limit order lets you wait for your price instead of taking whatever is on the board. Your credits stay set aside until the order fills or you cancel it. Patience can cost you a trade too, so set a time to review your orders.

**Keep some credits in reserve.** Keep part of your credits free. A reserve lets you take a good chance when it comes, and it pays the printing and auction fees that come due. Do not spend every credit you have on one trade.

## Supply and Demand
*How prices move, and why the market sets them*

**What sets a price.** A price is what a buyer will pay and a seller will accept for one lot. When more people want an item than there is for sale, the price tends to rise. When there is more for sale than people want, the price tends to fall. Merchants have watched this happen for thousands of years.

**Supply.** Supply is how much of a good sellers are willing to offer at a given price. In the game, supply is the ore that players bring in from the debris fields and put up for sale. More miners bring more ore to market, and the price has more to answer to.

**Demand.** Demand is how much buyers want at a given price. A player who needs iron ore for a build will bid for it. When many buyers want the same item at once, their bids climb. Prices move because what people want moves, not because someone decides.

**Prices on the Starship.** The Starship market sets the price. Every item starts from a set price, and every item has a floor. Nothing trades below its floor. Each trade sets the price for the next one, so the price changes as players buy and sell. The Starship bank trades on this market too.

**Prices lean back.** The bank keeps a standing bid 10% under and a standing ask 10% over the last price, so you can always sell and always buy. Prices also lean back toward a reference price for each item. A sudden rush of buyers or sellers can move a price, but the market gives it a pull back toward normal.

**The same idea everywhere.** In the real world, a price drops when a market fills with goods, and it rises when a shortage bites. Economists call the two sides supply and demand. The same idea works in a farm market, on a trading floor, or on a space station.

# Science · Research Assistant

## Elements and Compounds
*Atoms, and how to read the formula of a mineral*

**Everything is made of atoms.** Every material is built from tiny particles called atoms. About 118 different elements are known, and every mineral in the fields is made from some of them. Atoms are far too small to see, but chemists count and weigh them with great care.

**Elements: one kind of atom.** An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. Native gold, written Au, is an element, and so is pure carbon, which is the diamond in the fields. Iron, sulfur and copper are elements too.

**Compounds: atoms joined together.** A compound is two or more elements joined together by chemical bonds. Most ore minerals are compounds. Chalcopyrite, written CuFeS2, is made of copper, iron and sulfur joined into one crystal.

**Reading a formula.** The letters in a formula are the elements, and a small number after a letter counts its atoms. No number means one atom. So CuFeS2 has one copper atom, one iron atom and two sulfur atoms, and galena, PbS, has one lead atom for each sulfur atom.

**Share by weight.** Each kind of atom has a known atomic weight. Add up the weights in the formula, then one element's share is its weight divided by the total. For chalcopyrite, copper is about 34.6 per cent of the total weight, so 100 kg of this ore gives about 34.6 kg of copper. The rest is slag.

**What this means in the game.** A bigger share means more metal from each kilo of ore. Hematite, written Fe2O3, is about 70 per cent iron by weight, which is why it is such a useful iron ore. Every ore piece in the game is pure mineral, so its formula tells you its yield.

## Measuring Things
*Metres, kilograms, seconds, and how dense a mineral really is*

**Why we agree on units.** A measurement is only useful if everyone uses the same units. A metre means the same length to a miner, a pilot and a scientist, so their numbers can be compared. The game uses the same metric units, so every size and weight you read can be checked.

**The three base units.** The metre measures length. The kilogram measures mass, which is how much matter something holds. The second measures time. Each is now fixed by a physical fact: the metre by the speed of light, the kilogram by a fixed value of the Planck constant, and the second by the steady vibration of a caesium atom.

**Mass is not the same as weight.** Mass is how much stuff is in an object, and it is the same wherever you take it. Weight is the pull of gravity on that mass. A kilogram of ore has the same mass aboard the Starship as it does at a debris field, even if the pull of gravity is not the same.

**Density is mass per volume.** Density tells you how heavy a material is for its size. You find it by dividing the mass by the volume, and it is written in kilograms per cubic metre. Ice floats on water because ice has a lower density than water, which is 1,000 kg per cubic metre.

**Real densities in the fields.** Every mineral in the debris fields has its own real density. Basalt rock is about 2,900 kg per cubic metre, iron-nickel metal about 7,900, and native gold about 19,300. So 10 litres of gold, which is 0.01 cubic metres, weighs about 193 kg, while the same size of basalt weighs about 29 kg.

**Check your units.** Most mistakes come from mixing units. There are 1,000 litres in one cubic metre, so divide the litres by 1,000 before you use a density. Then ask whether the answer makes sense: a block of gold must be heavier than a block of basalt of the same size.

## The Scientific Method
*How to find out what is true, one question at a time*

**Start with a question.** Every experiment starts with a question you can test. "How much copper does 100 kg of chalcopyrite give?" is a good question, because you can weigh the result. "Is space nice?" is not, because nobody can measure it. A good question has an answer you can check with your hands or your instruments.

**Make a clear guess.** A hypothesis is a clear guess that could turn out to be wrong. Write it down before you test anything, for example: "About a third of the weight of this ore will be copper." Because it can be wrong, a hypothesis is useful. A guess that can never be proved wrong tells you nothing.

**Test one thing at a time.** In an experiment you change one thing and keep everything else the same. The part you keep the same is the control, and it lets you compare. If you change the sample, the furnace and the weight all at once, you will not know which one made the difference.

**Write down what you see.** Observations are what you notice while you work: the numbers on the scale, the colour, the time, the temperature. Write them down as they happen, using the same tool each time. Describe what you saw before you decide what it means.

**Reach a conclusion.** A conclusion says whether the evidence supports your hypothesis. It must match what you measured, no more and no less. If it does not match, the guess was wrong, or the test was poor, and both are worth knowing.

**Test it again.** A result you can repeat is one you can trust. Another scientist, or you tomorrow, should be able to follow the same method and get the same result. A result that cannot be repeated is set aside until someone can show it again.

## The Lab Notebook and Safety
*Recording what you see honestly, and staying safe in the lab*

**Write it down at once.** Record each observation as it happens, with the date, the time, what you did and the numbers you read. Memory fades and shifts, but a notebook keeps what you actually saw. Write in ink, and cross out mistakes with a single line so they can still be read.

**Record the results that go wrong.** A failed test is still a result. Write down what went wrong, what you changed and what happened next. Leaving out a failure makes the next person waste the same time, and it hides something you may need to know.

**Repeatability.** Run the test again with the same method, and write down each run. If the results match, you can trust them more. If they differ, look for the reason before you claim anything, such as a different sample, a different scale or a change in temperature.

**Honest results.** Report the numbers you got, how sure you are of them, and what you did not measure. Never change a number to match what you expected. Anyone who reads your notes depends on them being true.

**Basic lab safety.** Wear eye protection and gloves when the job needs them. Label every container, and never taste or sniff a sample to find out what it is. Keep food and drink out of the lab, and know where the exits and the first-aid kit are.

**Handling samples safely.** Read the label before you open a container. Never mix chemicals unless you know exactly what will happen. Some minerals, such as uraninite, are radioactive, so keep them in their own labelled box and ask before you handle them.

## The Wormhole: What We Know
*What the Starship has seen, and what is still unknown*

**Where it is.** The wormhole lies about 3 million km beyond Jupiter, on the side away from the Sun. It is far beyond the gas giants and the rings, so no ordinary craft goes there by chance. The Starship has to be steered to it.

**The Starship's station.** The Starship is parked about 30 km off the wormhole, with its bow turned toward the Sun. From this station its research teams can study it. Nothing in the record says what they will find, and the teams keep their notes as they go.

**What can be seen.** The throat is ringed with light. Through it you see another universe, whose stars are a different colour from ours and look denser and brighter. Its centre is never dark: it is no black hole, but a throat onto another universe.

**What a wormhole is in physics.** In physics, a wormhole is a theoretical bridge through space-time, a shortcut that some solutions of Einstein's equations allow. The idea goes back to 1935. No wormhole has ever been observed, so it remains a theory, not a fact.

**What we do not know.** We do not know how a throat like this could form, whether it would stay open, or what lies on the far side. Physicists who have studied traversable wormholes think they would need a kind of exotic matter that has never been found. These are open questions.

**Keep it honest.** Good scientists write "unknown" when they do not know. Everything the game says about the throat, its stars and the other universe is story. The physics is theory. Keep the two apart in your notes.

## Reading a Blueprint
*How a builder reads a design: its purpose, its parts, its weight and its serial*

**A blueprint is a plan for building.** A blueprint is a written design that someone else can build exactly. Read it the way a builder would: before you touch a material, check what the design is for, what it needs and what it will weigh. Every approved blueprint has been through the review team.

**What it is, and why.** Start with the name, the kind (a part, a tool or machine, a ship, or something else) and the problem it solves. Then check whether it is brand new, an upgrade, or a newer version or model. An upgrade or newer version names what it builds on and says what is different, so read that line closely.

**What it does, fits and improves.** These answers tell you the job. "Does" lists each thing the design does, in detail. "Fits" says what it attaches to: a ship, a machine, or nothing at all. "Improves" says what gets better, and by how much.

**The books it requires.** A design lists the library books it needs, so a builder knows what to learn first. Read that list before you start, and check which books you have already studied. Books are the knowledge behind the steps, not the parts themselves.

**Weight and size from the materials.** Each part lists its materials in kilograms. The weight is the total of those amounts. The space they take up is each amount divided by its density, added up: 1 kg of aluminium takes about 0.00037 m³, while 1 kg of iron takes about 0.00013 m³. A design can have up to 30 parts, each with up to 20 materials.

**Steps, time and serial numbers.** How it is built gives the tools, skills and numbered steps. Build time is in minutes, hours or days, up to a year, and builds run in real time. An approved design has a model code, such as BP-K7Q3X. Its original is BP-K7Q3X-ORIGINAL, and each printed copy carries its run and copy number, such as BP-K7Q3X-R2-0025.

## Forces and Structures
*Tension, compression, shear and bending, and why a triangle holds its shape*

**A force pushes or pulls.** A force is a push or a pull. Every structure, from a bicycle frame to a bridge, is built to carry forces without moving more than it should. Forces are measured in newtons. On Earth, a 1 kg mass pulls down on its support with about 9.8 newtons.

**Tension and compression.** Tension stretches a member, like a rope or a cable. Compression squeezes it, like a column holding up a roof. Cables are used where things are pulled, and columns where things are pushed. Stone and concrete are strong in compression but weak in tension.

**Shear and bending.** Shear is when two parts of a member slide past each other, like the blades of scissors cutting paper. Bending happens when a beam is loaded across its length: its top is squeezed while its bottom is stretched. A shelf sags under a row of books for exactly this reason.

**Why a triangle holds its shape.** Three bars joined at their ends form a triangle, and it cannot change shape unless one of its bars changes length. A four-sided frame can lean over into a diamond with no bar changing length at all. Add one diagonal bar and the square becomes two triangles, which is why frames are braced that way.

**Trusses and towers.** A truss is a framework of triangles. Each bar carries either tension or compression, and together the bars spread a heavy load across the whole frame. The Eiffel Tower is an open lattice of wrought iron, and the Forth Bridge in Scotland, opened in 1890, is a steel truss bridge.

**Arches and cables.** An arch carries its load mostly in compression, pushing the stones together. That is why old stone arch bridges have lasted for centuries. A suspension bridge hangs its deck from cables in tension. Choose the shape that suits the forces it must carry.

## Materials and Their Strengths
*Metals, ceramics and plastics: how strong, stiff and heavy each one is*

**Three families of material.** Most of what engineers build from is one of three families. Metals, such as iron, copper and aluminium, can be bent and shaped, and they usually bend before they break. Ceramics, such as brick, tile and glass, are very hard but brittle. Plastics are light and easy to shape.

**Strength, stiffness and weight.** Strength is how much load a material can take before it breaks or bends for good. Stiffness is how much it resists bending or stretching under the same load. Weight is density: the mass of one cubic metre. A good design balances all three, and it rarely maximises just one.

**Metals.** Iron is 7,874 kg per cubic metre in the Raw Materials Catalogue, and steel, which is mostly iron, is about the same. Aluminium is about 2,700, roughly a third of steel, which is why it is used where weight matters. Titanium, at about 4,507, is strong for its weight and resists rust. Metals are tough, which means they can take a sharp blow and still hold together.

**Ceramics: hard and heat-proof.** Ceramics such as brick, fired clay and glass stay strong at temperatures that would soften most metals, and they do not rust. Many are stiffer than steel. Their weak point is brittleness: they handle squeezing well, but they crack under a sudden blow or a pull. Basalt, the dark volcanic rock in our debris fields, is about 2,900 kg per cubic metre and is also used as a building stone.

**Plastics: light and easy to shape.** Plastics are long chains of molecules. Polyethylene, the plastic in bags and bottles, is about 950 kg per cubic metre, so it floats on water. Plastics are cheap and easy to mould, but they are far less stiff than metal, and many soften when they get hot.

**Choosing for the job.** Pick the material for what the part must do. A part that moves wants low weight and enough strength. A part that carries a load wants strength and stiffness. A part that sits near heat wants a material that stays strong when hot. Work out the weight from the densities before you build, not after.

## Measuring and Tolerances
*Units, precision and the small gaps that decide whether a part fits*

**One set of units.** Every measurement needs a unit, and everyone on a job must use the same one. The metre is the standard unit of length, the kilogram the unit of mass, and the second the unit of time. The blueprint form works in kilograms, so write your amounts the same way.

**Accuracy and precision.** Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value. Precision is how finely you can read it, and how well repeated readings agree. A ruler marked in millimetres can be read to about half a millimetre by eye. A micrometer can read to a hundredth of a millimetre.

**Tolerance.** A tolerance is how much a size may vary and still work. A shaft drawn at 20.00 mm with a tolerance of plus or minus 0.05 mm can measure anywhere from 19.95 to 20.05 mm and still fit. Tight tolerances cost more to make, so engineers set them only where a part needs them.

**Measure twice, cut once.** Check every measurement before you cut or drill. Mark clearly with a sharp pencil or scriber, measure from one reference edge, and check that your square is square. Write the number down and read it back, which catches most mistakes.

**Allowing for the cut and the heat.** A saw blade removes a little material, called the kerf, so leave it out of your count and every cut comes up short. Metal also grows when hot. Steel grows about 12 millionths of its length for each degree Celsius, so a 1 m steel bar grows about 0.012 mm for each degree.

**Real practice.** Working engineers check their measuring tools against a known standard, measure at the temperature the part will really be used, and record every result. Write each number with its unit, and round only at the very end.

## Workshop Safety
*Protective gear, tools, heat, pressure and electricity: the habits that keep hands whole*

**Plan before you start.** A workshop is safe when people plan before they begin. Look at the job, check the tools and the work area, and ask what could go wrong. Most accidents come from rushing, from skipping a check, or from a messy floor.

**Protective gear.** Wear safety glasses or goggles whenever something could fly, splash or shatter. Wear ear protection near loud machines, and a dust mask or respirator around dust and fumes. Tie back long hair, and do not wear loose clothing, jewellery or gloves near anything that spins.

**Hand tools and power tools.** Keep blades sharp and tools in good repair, because a dull blade needs more force and slips more. Use the guards on machines, and keep your fingers clear of moving parts. Unplug a power tool before you change a blade or bit, and never carry it by its cord.

**Heat and hot material.** Hot metal looks the same as cold metal, so treat anything that has been heated as hot until it has cooled. Use tongs and heat-proof gloves, and keep flammable things away from heat. Make sure the air moves freely where heat or fumes are made, and keep a fire extinguisher close by, knowing how to use it.

**Pressure and gas.** Compressed air can drive dust or chips into skin or eyes, so never point an air line at a person. Check hoses for damage before use. Gas cylinders and pressure vessels should be secured upright, and everyone should know where the shut-off valve is.

**Electricity and water.** Keep power tools and cords dry and in good repair. Where you work with mains power outdoors or near water, use a residual current device (RCD in the UK, GFCI in the US), which cuts the power when it detects a fault. Never work on live wiring. If something smells hot, sparks or trips the breaker, switch it off and have it checked.

## The Rarity Bands
*From common rock to the ultra-rare finds, band by band*

**Bands, not just minerals.** Every piece belongs to a rarity band, and the band decides which mineral it can be. There are nine bands, from the most common to the most unusual. A piece first lands in a band, then its mineral is picked from the few minerals that band holds.

**Common and plentiful.** The common band holds three minerals: basalt, iron-nickel metal and water ice. In a small field, about 40 pieces in every 100 are common. The plentiful band holds five, including iron ore, quartz and olivine, and in a small field about 22 in every 100.

**Ore and scarce.** The ore band holds eleven everyday ores, such as copper ore, lead ore, zinc ore and lithium ore. The scarce band holds nine, including silver ore, uraninite, cassiterite, native gold and industrial diamond.

**Rare, shock and shock-rare.** The rare band holds native platinum, native palladium and native iridium: metals found already as metal. The shock band holds four minerals that, on Earth, form under extreme shock, pressure or heat. The shock-rare band holds two more of the same kind, stishovite and ringwoodite.

**Lonsdaleite and brownleeite.** Two bands hold one mineral each. The lonsdaleite band holds lonsdaleite, and the brownleeite band holds brownleeite. These are the most unusual pieces in the fields, and the game gives each one its own alert when you collect it.

**Small fields hide more rare finds.** As fields get smaller, the common band takes a smaller share, and the rare bands take a bigger one. In a large field about 1 piece in 500 is rare metal. In a tiny field it is about 1 in 60. Brownleeite turns up only in tiny fields, at about 1 piece in 10,000.

## Reading a Debris Field
*Field sizes, piece counts, and how far away you can spot them*

**What a field is.** A debris field is a cloud of broken rock and ore, drifting together in one place. Each field has a size, and the size tells you what to expect: how many pieces there are and how big they are. It does not tell you what any one piece holds. That is worked out piece by piece.

**Four sizes.** There are four field sizes: large, medium, small and tiny. A large field holds 400 to 1,200 pieces, a medium one 60 to 250, a small one 10 to 20, and a tiny one 8 to 20. Medium fields are the most common, at about 35 in every 100.

**How big the pieces are.** Each piece has a scale in metres, and its box is about 1.7 times that scale on each side. Large fields have scales from 0.05 to 4.0. Medium fields run from 0.06 to 2.0, small fields from 0.12 to 1.2, and tiny fields from 0.04 to 0.35.

**How far you can spot a field.** Your scanner picks up a field from a distance that depends on its size. A large field shows up from about 1,100 metres away, a medium one from 600 metres, a small one from 260 metres, and a tiny one from 160 metres. Bigger fields can be found from farther off.

**Reading a field before you go.** A big field holds more pieces, so it holds more of every kind, rare ones too, even though any single piece is less likely to be rare. A tiny field has few pieces and is quick to clear. Use the size as a guide, not a promise: the pieces inside are still a roll.

## Rare Finds
*The near-flawless diamond, lonsdaleite and brownleeite, and what they really are*

**What makes a find rare.** Most pieces are common rock, ore or metal. A rare find comes from the rarest bands, and it turns up only once in a long while. The game gives three of these finds their own alert when you collect them, so you know you have one.

## Fitting the Hold
*What fits in the starter hold, and how much room each piece takes*

**The starter hold.** The starter hold is a box measuring 1.6 by 1.2 by 1.0 metres. That gives it 1.92 cubic metres of room for everything you carry. Every piece you take aboard has to fit inside that space.

**Shape: will it fit at all?.** A piece comes aboard only if its shape fits, turned any way you like. Its longest side must fit the hold's longest side, 1.6 metres, its middle side the middle, 1.2 metres, and its shortest side the shortest, 1.0 metre. A piece too long for the hold stays out, however much room is left.

**Room: its box, not its rock.** Room is counted by the piece's box: the box that holds the piece along its three main directions. A piece uses the room of its whole box, length times width times height, not just the rock inside. A piece is taken only if the boxes already aboard, plus its own box, still fit in 1.92 cubic metres.

**Why irregular rocks waste space.** A rock is not a box, so the box around it has empty corners. In the game, a piece's own volume is about half of its box, which means each piece takes about twice the room the rock itself fills. The hold counts the box, so every piece pays this cost, whatever its shape.

**What happens while you collect.** When you collect, the game takes the nearest piece that fits both tests, its shape and its box. Any piece that does not fit is skipped and left in the field, and the others are still taken. When nothing else fits, the game says the hold is full.

**Choosing what to carry.** Because room is limited, pick the pieces worth the space. A small, valuable piece can be worth more room than a large, light one. When you unload at the Starship, your hold moves into your own space in the Starship's cargo hold, which also has a fixed size.

## Size, Density and Weight
*How much a piece weighs, from its size and its real density*

**Weight from two numbers.** A piece's weight is its volume multiplied by its density. Volume is how much space the rock fills. Density is how heavy one cubic metre of that material is. Multiply the two, and you have the mass in kilograms.

**Volume from size.** Each piece is shaped like a twenty-sided solid, an icosahedron. Its volume is about 2.5 times its scale multiplied by itself three times. Every piece is stretched a little differently along its three directions, so you multiply all three together. No two pieces are exactly the same.

**Real densities.** Densities are in kilograms per cubic metre. Water ice is 917, basalt 2,900, quartz 2,650, copper ore 4,190, lead ore 7,600, native gold 19,300, and native iridium 22,560, the densest piece in the fields.

**A worked example: quartz.** Take a piece with a scale of 0.2 metres. Its volume is 2.5 times 0.2 times 0.2 times 0.2, which is about 0.020 cubic metres. Quartz weighs 2,650 kilograms per cubic metre, so 0.020 times 2,650 is about 53 kilograms.

**The same size, very different weight.** Take the same 0.2 metre piece and make it from different materials. As water ice it weighs about 19 kilograms, as quartz about 54, and as native gold about 390. Gold weighs about twenty times as much as ice of the same size.

**Why weight matters.** Weight and size come from the real densities, and the game never lets you type them in. The market sells ore in lots by weight, so the kilograms you carry are the kilograms you can sell. Check the weight before you decide what to take.

# Medicine · Medic

## How the Body Works
*The heart, lungs, muscles, brain and digestion, and how they work together*

**Many systems, one body.** Your body is a set of systems that work together. Each one has its own job, and none of them can do its work alone. Knowing what each system does is the first step to knowing how to keep yourself well.

**Heart and blood.** The heart is a muscle that pumps blood around the body through a network of tubes called blood vessels. The blood carries oxygen and food to every cell and takes away waste. The heart keeps beating all your life, and it never has to be told to start.

**Lungs and breathing.** The lungs take in air through the nose and mouth. Tiny air sacs in the lungs pass oxygen into the blood, while carbon dioxide passes out to be breathed away. Breathing is mostly automatic, but you can slow it down or speed it up when you choose.

**Muscles and bones.** Bones form a frame that holds the body up and protects delicate parts such as the heart and brain. Muscles are fixed to bones, and when they shorten they pull the bones and make you move. Bones also make new blood cells inside their marrow.

**Brain and nerves.** The brain is the control centre. It takes in messages from your senses, decides what to do, and sends signals along the nerves to your muscles. Nerves are long fibres that carry those signals quickly around the body.

**Digestion.** Digestion breaks food down into small pieces the body can use. The stomach and intestines absorb the useful parts into the blood, and the liver helps to process them. Whatever the body cannot use leaves as waste.

## First Aid Principles
*Stay calm, check for danger, call for help, and know your limits*

**Stay calm.** Panic makes everything harder. Take a breath, look around and think before you act. A calm person can notice danger, judge what is happening and get help.

**Check for danger first.** Before you go to anyone, make sure the area is safe for you too. A rescuer who is hurt cannot help anyone. Move away from fire, smoke, leaks or anything else that could cause more harm.

**Call for help.** Get help quickly. On Earth that means the local emergency number; aboard a ship, the crew in charge of medical care. Say clearly what happened, where it happened and how many people are hurt.

**Keep the person warm.** Cold can make an injured or unwell person worse. Cover them with a blanket or spare clothing, keep them comfortable and talk to them calmly while you wait for help.

**Do not move someone without need.** Moving an injured person can make the injury worse, especially to the neck or back. Move them only if they are in danger and cannot be kept safe where they are. Otherwise, leave the moving to trained people.

**Know your limits.** Good first aid means doing only what you have been trained to do. Learn the basics on a proper course, and keep a well-stocked first-aid kit where people can reach it. When in doubt, asking for trained help is always the right choice.

## Nutrition Basics
*What food gives the body, and why sugar burns bright and fast*

**What food is for.** Food gives your body energy and the materials it needs to build and repair itself. No single food does everything. A healthy body needs a mix of several kinds of nutrients, and each one has its own job.

**Carbohydrates.** Carbohydrates are found in grains, fruit, vegetables and sugar, and they are the body's quickest source of energy. The body breaks them down into a sugar called glucose, and the blood carries that glucose to the cells that need it.

**Protein.** Protein is built from smaller parts called amino acids. The body uses it to build and repair muscle, skin and many other parts. Eggs, beans, fish, nuts and meat are all common sources of protein.

**Fats.** Fats store energy for later and help the body take in some vitamins. Gram for gram, fat holds more energy than carbohydrates or protein. The body needs some fat to work well, and the kinds of fat matter.

**Vitamins, minerals and water.** Vitamins and minerals are needed in small amounts for many jobs in the body. Water carries nutrients, keeps your temperature steady and helps every cell work. About 60% of an adult body is water, which is why thirst is a signal to drink.

**Why sugar gives a quick burst.** Sugar is digested very fast, so the level of sugar in the blood rises quickly and gives a sharp burst of energy. The body responds by releasing insulin, which moves the sugar into the cells, and the level can then drop fast. That drop is why a sugary snack can leave you tired soon after. Slower carbohydrates, such as oats and whole grains, release energy more evenly.

## Sleep and Recovery
*What sleep does for the body and the brain, and how your three bars refill*

**What sleep is for.** Sleep is when the body repairs muscle and tissue, and when the brain sorts and stores what you learned during the day. Most adults need about 7 hours or more a night. Feeling fine on less is rare, and sleep debt builds up quietly.

**Sleep cycles.** A night of sleep moves through cycles of about 90 minutes. Each cycle has light sleep, deep sleep and dream sleep. Deep sleep does most of the physical repair, and dream sleep helps with memory and mood.

**Your three bars.** Aboard the Starship your body has three bars: stamina, energy and mental focus. While you are awake, each one drains by 2.2 points an hour. That means a 16-hour day takes you from 100% down to 65%.

**When the bars fall.** 65% is the lowest recommended level. Near the top of the scale your abilities slip only slightly. Below 65% they decline severely, and below 50% they decline extremely, so studying and guiding the ship become much harder. On-screen warnings remind you when you are pushing past healthy rest.

**Resting in bed.** In bed, sleep refills each bar by 35 points every 6 hours, about 5.8 points an hour. From 65%, 6 hours takes you back to 100%. From 50%, 6 hours reaches only 85%, and a full rest takes about 8½ hours. Sleeping in the cockpit out in space refills at half that rate, so the bed is always the best rest.

**Real rest, real time.** The game runs in real time, just as the real world does. A nap does not speed up a job that takes weeks. A steady sleep time helps the body clock stay in step, so try to rest at the same hours each day.

## The Body in Space
*How low gravity, fluid shifts and radiation change the body, and how crews keep well*

**Built for gravity.** The human body was shaped by life on Earth, where gravity pulls on it all day long. Muscles and bones are kept in shape by that steady pull. Take the pull away, and the body starts to change.

**Muscle loss.** In low gravity the muscles that hold posture and move the legs have less work to do, so they get smaller and weaker over time. Daily strength exercise is the main way crews keep their muscles in good shape.

**Bone loss.** Bones stay strong partly because of the load they carry. In space, bones lose some of their mineral and density, most in the legs and hips, and the loss continues for as long as the body stays in low gravity. Regular strength exercise helps slow it.

**Fluid shifts.** On Earth, gravity pulls body fluid down into the legs. In space, fluid drifts up toward the head and chest. Many people notice a puffy face and a stuffy nose at first, and the body slowly adjusts over several days.

**Radiation.** Beyond Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field, space carries more radiation from the Sun and from cosmic rays. Ships reduce the dose with shielding, such as thick walls and stored supplies including water, and with careful planning of how long people spend in the most exposed places.

**Sleep, rhythm and exercise.** The body keeps a daily rhythm called the circadian rhythm, set mostly by light. A ship may have no sunrise, so crews keep a steady sleep schedule and a set daily routine. On the International Space Station, astronauts exercise about two hours a day to fight muscle and bone loss.

# Computer Science · Programmer

## Algorithms
*Step-by-step instructions, sorting, searching and why order matters*

**A recipe for a computer.** An algorithm is a clear list of steps that solves a problem, and that works every time it is followed. A recipe is an algorithm, and so is a set of directions. A computer can only follow an algorithm exactly as it is written.

**Steps must be exact.** Each step has to be simple enough to do without guessing. "Find the biggest number" is not yet a good step; "look at each number in turn, and keep the largest one you have seen so far" is. A good algorithm also always reaches an end.

**Searching.** Suppose you need one name in a list of ten thousand. Checking each name from the top, one at a time, is called a linear search. It always works, but in the worst case it can take ten thousand checks.

**Halving the list.** If the list is sorted, you can check the middle name first. If it is the one you want, stop. If the name you want comes before the middle, throw away the second half and repeat on the first. This is binary search, and it needs only about 14 checks for ten thousand names.

**Sorting.** Sorting puts items in order, such as a list of prices from lowest to highest. A simple method called bubble sort swaps neighbours that are out of order and repeats until nothing moves. Smarter methods handle very large lists far faster.

**Why order matters.** Binary search only works on a sorted list, and on an unsorted one it can give the wrong answer. The order of steps changes results in general: taking a 10% discount before adding tax is not the same as adding tax first.

## Binary and Data
*Bits, bytes, and how text and pictures become numbers*

**Two states.** A computer's memory is made of billions of tiny switches, and each one is either on or off. We write on as 1 and off as 0. One switch holding one of these two values is called a bit.

**Counting in twos.** Binary is counting with only two digits, 0 and 1. Each place is worth double the place to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on. So binary 101 means 4, plus nothing for the 2, plus 1, which makes 5.

**Bytes.** Eight bits together make one byte. A single byte can hold 256 different values, from 0 up to 255. Bigger amounts are counted in kilobytes, megabytes and gigabytes, each one roughly a thousand times the last.

**Text as numbers.** Every letter is given a number. In the common ASCII code, a capital A is 65, which is 01000001 in binary. Modern text uses a larger system called Unicode, which covers every alphabet and many symbols.

**Pictures as numbers.** A picture is a grid of tiny dots called pixels. Each pixel's colour is stored as three numbers, for red, green and blue, and each number runs from 0 to 255. Mixing those three gives about 16 million colours.

**Why this matters.** Everything a computer keeps, from a letter to a price or a receipt code, is kept as numbers in this same way. The machine only ever sees bits. What they mean depends on how the program reads them.

## What a Computer Does
*Input, processing, memory, storage and output*

**A machine that follows steps.** A computer takes in information, works on it and gives back a result. It does this by following instructions called a program. Everything you do on a computer, from a game to a spreadsheet, is this same loop.

**Input.** Input is how information gets in: a keyboard, a mouse, a touchscreen, a microphone or a sensor. A scanner reading the sky is input too. Input only brings the data in; it does not do the thinking.

**Processing.** The processor, also called the CPU, is the part that does the work. It carries out simple operations such as adding two numbers, comparing two values and moving data from one place to another. A modern chip can do billions of these every second.

**Memory.** Memory, usually called RAM, holds the data and the program the processor is using right now. It is very fast, but it is cleared when the power goes off. Think of it as the desk you are working at, not the filing cabinet.

**Storage.** Storage keeps information even when the power is off. A hard drive, a solid-state drive or a memory card are all storage. It is slower than memory, but it holds far more, and saved files live there.

**Output.** Output is how results get out: a screen, a speaker or a printer. The whole cycle is input, processing with memory and storage, then output, and a modern computer can run it many times in a fraction of a second.

## Debugging and Testing
*Finding and fixing mistakes, and checking that they are really fixed*

**Bugs are normal.** A bug is a mistake in a program that makes it do something other than what was meant. Every programmer makes them, and finding them is a normal part of the work. The word is old: one famous early case, in 1947, was a real moth stuck in a computer.

**Find it step by step.** Start by writing down what should happen and what actually happens. Then check the values at a few points in the program, and find the first point where they go wrong. The bug is usually at that point or just before it.

**Test small pieces.** Test one small part at a time, using inputs where you already know the right answer. This is called unit testing. A small piece that passes every test you give it can be trusted before you build more on top of it.

**Try the edges.** Many bugs hide at the edges: an empty list, the number zero, the largest possible value, or a value just over a limit. A good test tries these as well as the easy, typical case.

**Why checks matter.** A check proves that something is what it claims to be. In the game, a receipt code can be looked up on the receipt check, which shows what was recorded without showing who owns it. A blueprint serial works the same way, and it is genuine only if the server issued it.

**Fixing without breaking.** After a fix, run the tests again. A change in one place can break something far away, and rerunning the tests catches that. Write down what you changed and why, so anyone reading later can understand the fix.

## Logic and Decisions
*True and false, AND, OR, NOT, if, then and loops*

**True and false.** Logic is reasoning with statements that are either true or false, with nothing in between. "The hold is full" is either true or false. Computers make every decision by checking statements like this one.

**AND.** AND is true only when both statements are true. "The craft has fuel AND the hatch is closed" is true only when both parts hold. If either part is false, the whole statement is false.

**OR.** OR is true when at least one of the statements is true, and also when both are. "The bank has credit OR the market has a buyer" is true if either part is true, so only a statement with both parts false fails.

**NOT.** NOT turns a statement around. If "the thruster is on" is true, then NOT "the thruster is on" is false. NOT is the simplest of the three, but it is needed everywhere a rule says "unless".

**If, then and otherwise.** A decision is written as: if a condition is true, do this, otherwise do that. A game might say: if a body bar is below 50, the character is impaired; otherwise carry on as normal. Every choice a program makes is built from this.

**Loops.** A loop repeats steps while a condition is true, or a set number of times, or once for each item in a list. A loop must have a way to end. A loop that never ends is called an infinite loop, and it is a common kind of bug.

# Navigation & Cartography · Navigator

## Bearings and Triangulation
*Compass bearings, crossing lines, and how the scanner fixes a field*

**What a bearing is.** A bearing is the direction to something, given as an angle. Navigators count from north and turn clockwise: north is 0 degrees, east is 90, south is 180 and west is 270. A bearing of 45 degrees points north-east.

**One bearing is only a line.** A single bearing tells you which way a thing lies, but not how far away it is. The thing could be anywhere along that straight line. To find where it really is, you need a second bearing from somewhere else.

**Two lines, one place.** Take a bearing on the same thing from a second place. The two lines cross at one point, and that point is where the thing is. This method is called triangulation, because the two places and the thing make a triangle.

**The scanner's direction.** The game scanner works the same way. One sweep tells you which way a debris field lies, but not how far it is. When you take a bearing, the game remembers the spot where you stood, and draws the line from there.

**How far to move.** The second bearing needs a real move to one side. The game asks for more than 35 metres, or more than about 12 per cent of the distance to the field, whichever is greater. The new bearing must also point at a clearly different angle, by more than about 2.5 degrees.

**Why the angle matters.** Two lines that run almost side by side cross very far away, and a small error in either one moves the crossing point a long way. A wide angle between the two bearings gives a sharper fix. Surveyors and sailors both plan their bearings with this in mind.

## Charting the Rings
*The band around each ring, how the game checks it, and the radar as a chart*

**What a chart is for.** A chart shows where things are. It needs a scale, so you know how far apart they are, and a way to read direction. A good chart lets you plan a route before you set out, instead of guessing as you go.

**A ring's line and its band.** Each ring is a circle of debris around the Sun, at the distance where its planet once orbited. The debris does not sit on one exact line. So the game marks a band around each line, reaching 25 km to either side and 6 km above and below the plane of the orbits.

**How the game checks a band.** The game measures your distance from the Sun's axis, leaving out your height, and compares it with each ring's distance. If the difference is under 25 km and you are less than 6 km above or below the plane, you are inside that ring's band. Otherwise you are between the rings.

**The radar as a chart.** The radar scope has four ranges: 5 km, 75 km, 2 AU and 11 AU. Each one is a different scale of the same chart. 5 km shows nearby fields, and 75 km is wide enough to show a whole ring band with room to spare on either side.

**Choosing a scale.** Real charts come in many scales. A street map is drawn at a large scale, so you see each shop. A chart of a whole country is drawn at a small scale, so you see the roads between cities. Pick the scale that shows what you need for the job.

**Keeping a chart up to date.** Charts are only as good as their latest survey. Navigators add what they find to the chart, so the next person knows where the hazards are. A careful navigator always checks the date on a chart before trusting it.

## Coordinates and Maps
*Latitude, longitude, map projections and the longitude shown in WARP*

**Two numbers for a place.** A map finds any place on Earth with two numbers. Latitude is how far north or south of the equator a place lies, from 0 degrees at the equator to 90 degrees at each pole. Longitude is how far east or west it lies, counted from the line through Greenwich in England, which is 0 degrees.

**Degrees, minutes and seconds.** A degree splits into 60 minutes, and a minute splits into 60 seconds. So a spot written as 51 degrees, 28 minutes and 40 seconds north is written 51°28′40″N. The smaller units let a navigator point to a place much more exactly than whole degrees could.

**A round Earth on a flat map.** A globe curves, and a flat map cannot show it without stretching something. Each projection chooses what to keep true. The Mercator map keeps directions true, which helped sailors, but it makes land near the poles look far too big. Other maps keep areas true and stretch the shapes instead.

**Longitude around the Sun.** The game uses the same idea around the Sun. The Sun sits at the centre, and the orbits lie flat around it like the ground. Each place has an angle round the Sun, measured from a fixed starting line that the game sets. It does not turn when your craft turns.

**What WARP shows you.** The WARP list shows each destination's longitude in degrees, from 0 up to 360. A ring is listed with the longitude you would arrive at. Warp to a ring and you arrive at the ring at the same angle round the Sun that you are at now, and only your distance from the Sun changes.

**Why everyone uses the same grid.** Ships, aircraft and satellites all use a grid of numbers like this. A place is only useful to others when everyone uses the same zero line and the same units. Once you agree on the grid, you can tell someone else exactly where to go.

## Plotting a Course
*Routes, bends around the Sun, and planning ahead like a real navigator*

**What a course is.** A course is the route from where you are to where you want to go. Real navigators plot it on a chart before they set off, then check their position as they travel. A good plan saves time and fuel.

**The straight line.** The shortest route between two points is a straight line. WARP follows a straight line whenever it can. A much longer trip takes only a little longer, because WARP's travel time grows slowly as the distance grows.

**When the Sun is in the way.** Sometimes the straight line passes close to the Sun. If it passes within 0.25 AU of the Sun, WARP does not cut through that space. Real missions keep well clear of the Sun too, because of its heat and its pull on the craft.

**The bend.** In that case the route swings out round the outside of the Sun, to a point at least 0.6 AU from it, on the side you are already on. Think of walking round a lake instead of straight across the middle of it.

**Planning ahead.** Planets keep moving, so a craft aimed at where a planet is now will miss it. Real mission planners aim at where the planet will be when the craft arrives. Good navigation is about the future position, not only the present one.

**Moving between orbits.** A real way to move from one orbit to another is the Hohmann transfer. It uses two engine burns, with a long coast between them along an oval path. It is one of the most fuel-efficient ways to move between near-circular orbits, which is why planners use it so often.

## Scale and Distance
*Map scales, metres, kilometres and AU, and the game at true scale*

**What a scale tells you.** A scale says how much real distance one unit of a drawing stands for. A map marked 1:100,000 means that one centimetre on paper is one kilometre on the ground. The bigger the second number, the more ground fits on the page, and the less detail you see.

**Metres, kilometres and the game.** The game measures everything in metres, so one unit in the game is one real metre. A kilometre is a thousand metres. The game writes the number that suits the distance: metres for the very short, kilometres for the nearby, and AU once the distances get very large.

**How the game writes a distance.** Under 1,000 metres the game shows whole metres. Up to 100 km it shows kilometres with one decimal place. Past that it shows whole kilometres, and once a distance passes about 7.5 million km it switches to AU, with two decimal places. The same distance always reads the same way, so you can compare the lists.

**True size, true distance.** The Sun has a true radius of about 695,700 km, and the rings and gas giants sit at their true distances from it. The numbers are exact to scale. Bodies farther than 200 km away are drawn closer in, but at their true apparent size, so they look exactly as large as they would from where you are.

**Far things look small.** In real life, a thing looks smaller the farther away it is. Move ten times farther from a debris field and it looks about one tenth as wide. That is why a distant object can look tiny even when it is huge, and why the labels give the real distance.

**Distance from speed.** The info line shows your speed in metres per second. Multiply the speed by the number of seconds you travel to get the distance you cover. At 60 metres a second, a craft covers 3.6 km in one minute.
