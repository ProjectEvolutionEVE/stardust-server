# The Cedar Drawer

*Working title only. Draft v0.1 · 2026-10-20 · for Eric's approval.*

## Chapter One: The Drawer

The drawer sticks.

It has stuck since the day they fitted my quarters, a fraction of a millimetre out of true, the kind of fault a
maintenance crew would fix in four minutes if I ever filed a ticket. I have never filed the ticket. I like that it
sticks. I like that I have to lift it a little and pull, the way you lift a door that has dropped on its hinges in an
old house, because it means the drawer cannot be opened by accident. It means that every time it opens, I meant it.

Tonight I meant it.

My shift in the Tower ended an hour ago. Nine small craft out, nine small craft home, and the last one came in a
little hot under the bow, so I talked it down slower than it wanted to go, and it docked, and the pilot's breathing
on the channel went from quick to ordinary, and I said what I always say, which is nothing much. *Welcome home.* Then
I handed the board to the night watch and walked the long curve of the corridor to my door, and sat on the edge of the
bed with my boots still on, and looked at the drawer for a while before I opened it.

The shirt is folded the way I folded it the first time, years ago, which is not well. One sleeve is tucked under
and the other lies across the top like an arm across a chest. The collar has worn through at the back, where a neck
rubbed it for a thousand days. There is a small tear by the third button that someone tried to mend with thread that
was not quite the right colour. I know who did that. It was me.

I do not take it out. I lean down, and I breathe.

Cedar.

Still. After everything. After a ship and a yard and a wormhole and a whole other world, after twelve months on the
far side and the long fall back into a sky that had been changed while we were away. Still cedar, faint as a voice in
another room, sweet and dry and a little sharp at the edges, like pencil shavings, like a wardrobe in a house where
the windows face south. The scent lives deep in the weave now. I have been told that is not possible, that scent
fades on a schedule as reliable as orbits. I have been told a lot of things.

I close the drawer. I lift it a little, and push, and it settles into its stuck place.

Then, because it is late, and because nobody can see, I take a square of chocolate from the tin behind the books on
my shelf and let it melt on my tongue without chewing, the way my grandmother taught me, and I think about where I
learned to love small lights in the dark.

---

I was seven the summer of the jar.

My grandmother lived at the edge of a town so small it had one road in and the same road out, and behind her house
the land fell away to a creek, and on the far side of the creek the cedars began. They were not tall the way forests
in pictures are tall. They were old and stubborn and close together, and when the wind came through them it did not
roar, it hushed, the way a person hushes a child. In the heat of the day you could smell them from the porch. In the
evening you could smell them from inside the house with all the doors closed.

I was sent to her for the summers. I did not understand why, then, and I understand it only partly now. There are
reasons adults give children and reasons adults keep, and I was the kind of child who could tell the difference and
was too polite to say so. What I knew was that my grandmother's house was quiet, that the floors creaked in a
particular order between the kitchen and the stairs, and that if I was very careful I could walk from my bed to the
back door without making a single board speak.

That was my first skill. Silence. Knowing exactly where to put my feet.

My grandmother had skills of her own. She could tell rain was coming by the way the screen door swung. She could
peel an apple in one long ribbon. And she had a tin, a flat dented tin with a faded picture of a ship on the lid,
which lived on the highest shelf of the pantry behind the flour, and which contained chocolate.

She never offered it. That was the rule. She did not hide it from me, exactly. She simply never mentioned it, and I
understood, in the way children understand the shape of things that are not said, that the tin was hers and that
its existence was a kind of privacy. Once, late, when I came down for water, I found her at the kitchen table in the
dark with the tin open in front of her and one square on her tongue, her eyes closed, not chewing. She opened her
eyes and looked at me. She did not put the tin away.

"You let it go slowly," she said. "If you chew it, it's over before you've met it."

She broke a square in half and gave me the smaller piece, and we sat in the dark together and did not chew. That was
the only time she shared the tin, and neither of us ever spoke of it again. I think that is why, to this day, I do
not offer mine. It is not meanness. It is that a secret shared once, in the right dark, is worth more than a sweet
handed round at a party. Some things are better for being kept.

---

The fireflies came in the second week of the summer.

I had never seen them before. Where my parents lived the nights were orange with streetlight and nothing blinked
except the red lamps on the radio masts. So the first evening I sat on the back step after supper and saw a small
green-gold light rise out of the long grass by the creek, I thought it was something in my eye. I rubbed my eye. It
went out. Then another one rose, further off, and hung, and went out, and then there were three, and then a dozen,
and then the whole slope down to the water was breathing with them, on and off, on and off, slow and soft and
impossibly many, as if the grass were full of tiny lanterns being carried by people I couldn't see.

I did not move. I think I held my breath until it hurt.

My grandmother came out behind me with a dish towel over her shoulder and stood there a while.

"They're talking," she said.

"To who?"

"To each other. Each one's saying, *here I am*. And waiting to see who says it back."

I watched them for a long time after that. I watched until the light was all gone from the sky and the fireflies
were the only light left, and then I watched them some more. I started to see that she was right. They were not
blinking at random. A light would flash low in the grass, and then, a breath later, another would answer higher up,
and then the first would flash again, a little closer. Here I am. Here I am. I'm here. I'm here.

I did not have words for it then. I have them now. They were calling each other in.

---

The jar was my idea, and I am not proud of it.

It was an ordinary jar, the kind my grandmother kept for jam, with a lid that screwed on. I asked her for it the
next morning and she gave it to me without asking what for, which was her way. I spent the afternoon punching holes
in the lid with a nail and a stone, because I had read somewhere that you had to, and that evening I went down to
the creek with the jar in both hands.

Catching them was harder than I thought. A firefly is only where it is when it is lit, and the moment you see it,
it has already gone dark, and in the dark it moves. I spent the first half hour lunging at places where lights had
been, and closing my hands on nothing, and getting my socks wet in the creek mud. Then I made myself stop. I stood
still in the grass, the way I stood still on the creaking floorboards, and I watched.

And I saw that each one had a path. Not a straight path, but a path all the same: it would flash, drift in the dark
for the space of a slow count, and flash again, and the second flash was always a little higher and a little to one
side of the first, as if it were climbing a staircase that only it could see. If you watched a single light for three
or four flashes, you could tell where the next one would be.

So I waited for the next one. I held the jar where it would be. And it flashed inside the glass.

I screwed the lid on. My hands were shaking.

I caught six that night. I know it was six because I counted, again and again, every time they lit: one, two, three,
four, five, six. I carried the jar up to my room and set it on the windowsill beside my bed and lay down and watched
it. In the jar they did not drift and climb. They crawled up the glass and slid down it and lit, and lit, and lit,
but there was nobody to answer them. The green-gold flashes hit the glass and stopped.

After a while I noticed that they were flashing faster. Not slower, as I'd expected, as if they were tired. Faster.
Here I am, here I am, here I am. Like someone knocking on a door. Like someone saying a name over and over into a
radio that isn't working.

I lay there in the dark and listened to them say it, and something in my stomach turned over slowly, the way it does
when you realise you have done a wrong thing that nobody is going to punish you for.

I got up. I didn't put on my shoes. I walked down the stairs, putting my feet in the silent places, and through the
kitchen, and out of the back door, and down the slope to the creek, with the jar held out in front of me like a lamp.
At the bottom, by the water, I unscrewed the lid.

For a moment none of them moved. Then one climbed to the rim and sat there, dark, and I thought perhaps I had hurt
it. Then it lit, and lifted, and drifted up out of the jar, and flashed again a little higher and a little to one side,
climbing its stair. And somewhere out in the grass, a breath later, something answered it.

The others followed, one by one. I counted them out. Six. I stood there with the empty jar and watched the sixth one
find its answer, and the slope went on breathing around me, thousands of lights, all of them calling, all of them being
called.

When I turned around, my grandmother was standing on the back step in her nightgown with a lamp in her hand. I don't
know how long she had been there. She didn't tell me off for being out in the dark without shoes. She just held the
lamp a little higher so I could see the path back up through the grass, and waited, and when I reached her she put
her hand on the top of my head for a moment, and then we went in.

In the morning she didn't ask for the jar back. She never mentioned it. It sat on my windowsill for the rest of the
summer, clean and empty, with its little nail-holes in the lid, and every night I looked at it before I looked out of
the window. I think she knew that I needed to keep it. Not to use. To remember.

That was the lesson of the jar, and it has taken me most of my life to put it into words. You can watch a light
without owning it. You can learn its path so well that you know where it will be before it knows. And the kindest
thing you can do with that knowledge, almost always, is to hold the way open and let it go home.

---

I didn't think about the jar for years. Children don't think about the things that shaped them; they are too busy
being shaped. I went home at the end of that summer and went to school and was not very good at it. I came back the
next summer, and the one after, and every year the fireflies came in the second week, and every year I sat on the step
and watched them call each other in. I never caught another one. I didn't need to. I had learned to follow a single
light with my eyes through a whole slope of them, through ten flashes, twenty, and to lose it only when it found what it
was looking for.

I thought it was a game. I didn't know it was a trade.

The first time I sat in a traffic chair, years later, with a headset on and a screen full of small moving lights, each
one a vessel, each one a person in a seat with their breathing on the channel, I felt something in my chest go very
still, and I knew I had been here before. Every craft out there was saying the same thing in its own way, in
transponder codes and position reports and the small catch in a voice that has been alone too long. *Here I am.* And
the whole job, the whole of it, underneath the procedures and the clearances and the checklists, was to say it back.
*I see you. I'm here. This way.*

That is what the Tower is. People think it is a room full of screens and rules. It is. But underneath the screens and
the rules, it is a slope at dusk, and a girl in wet socks, and a voice answering in the dark.

---

I am getting ahead of myself. A story should start where it starts, and this one, if I am honest, does not start with
the jar. It starts with the cedar.

The cedars were on the far side of the creek, and I was not allowed to cross it. That was the other rule of my
grandmother's house, as firm as the rule about the tin and spoken just as rarely. The creek was shallow, but the
bank on the far side was steep and the roots of the trees made a kind of wall, and beyond the wall the wood was dark
even at noon. She said it once, the first summer: *Not past the water.* And I obeyed it for four years, which, for me,
was a very long time to obey anything.

I would sit on the near bank and look across. In the heat the smell came over the water in waves, so strong some days
that it was nearly a taste. The cedars creaked when the wind moved them. Birds I couldn't name went in and out. And
sometimes, in the late afternoon, from somewhere deep in the trees, I heard a sound I couldn't place at all: a
rhythmic, patient rasp, like breathing, like someone slowly brushing a rug. It would go on for a while and stop, and
then start again.

I asked my grandmother about it. She was shelling peas and didn't look up.

"That'll be the woodshop," she said.

"What woodshop?"

"There's a woodshop over the hill. Past the trees. Someone works there."

"Who?"

She looked up then, and studied me for a moment in a way that made me feel she was reading something written on my
face that I hadn't put there on purpose.

"Not past the water," she said, and went back to her peas.

---

I crossed the water the summer I was eleven.

I'd like to say I had a reason. I didn't. It was hot and I was bored and the rasping sound had started up in the trees
and I had decided, the way children decide things, all at once and completely, that I would find out what it was. I
took off my shoes and tied the laces together and hung them round my neck, and I waded across where the creek was
widest and slowest, and I climbed the bank by the roots, and I went into the cedars.

It was cooler under them. The light came down green and broken. The ground was soft with needles and so quiet that
my own breathing sounded loud, and the smell was everywhere, not in waves now but all around, inside me, the way water
is all around you when you swim. I walked toward the sound. It stopped. I stood still, the way I'd learned to, and
waited. After a while it started again, and I walked on.

I came out at the top of a rise, where the trees thinned, and below me in a clearing there was a long low building
with its doors propped open, and stacks of planks under a tin roof, and a yard covered in pale curls of shaved wood
like a carpet of ribbons. The sound was coming from inside. I went down the slope as quietly as I could, which was
very quietly, and I stood in the doorway.

I am not going to tell you, yet, who I saw.

I will tell you that it was someone not much older than me, bent over a long board on two trestles, pushing a hand
plane along it in slow even strokes, and that each stroke made the sound I had heard from the creek, and that each
stroke lifted a curl of cedar so thin you could see light through it, and that the curl fell to the floor with all
the others. I will tell you that they didn't look up for a long time. I will tell you that when they did look up, they
didn't seem surprised to see a girl with her shoes round her neck and mud to the knees, standing very still in the
doorway. They looked at me the way my grandmother had looked at me over the peas, as if reading something.

Then they held out the plane, handle first.

"You push from your shoulders," they said, "not your hands."

I have kept that sentence for my whole life. I have kept it the way I keep the shirt, folded badly, in a place that
sticks a little, so that I only take it out when I mean to.

---

It is late now. The ship hums the way it always hums, a sound so constant that you only hear it when you're tired.
Down the corridor, the night watch has the board. Out past the bow there are rings of broken stone where there used to
be worlds, and among the stones there are small dark shapes that are not ours, sifting and sorting, and they do not
answer when we call. Further out, behind us, past Jupiter, there is the door we came home through, and beyond it a
green world that has never heard a human voice except ours.

People aboard ask each other what happened while we were gone. Everyone has a theory. I listen to them all and I
believe none of them yet, because none of them has been proven, and I have spent my life learning not to call a light
before it's lit. And people ask the other question too, quietly, in the galley, at the end of long shifts. *Did anyone
on the planet survive?* We don't know. That's the truth of it. We are Earth's people, every one of us aboard, and we are
alive, and beyond that the page is blank. I have seen what is left out there. I have also seen a single firefly find
its answer across a whole dark slope. The odds are dim. I don't say never.

So I'll tell it from the beginning. The creek and the cedars. The woodshop and the plane. The years in between, and the
long climb from the bottom rung, and the yard where a great ship waited to be finished, and the night the door opened
near Jupiter. I'll tell it the way I bring a small craft home: slowly, one flash at a time, holding the way open.

I lift the drawer a little, and pull, and it gives.

Cedar.

Here I am.
